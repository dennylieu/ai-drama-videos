# The ad underneath: direct response for song ads

Source: Zakaria, "Song Ads Are The New Trend. Most Of You Will Fail At Them, Because You Think The Song Is What
Sells", X article, 2026-11-01 (https://x.com/zakaria_airak/status/2116757094418330021). Summarised and rewritten
for this repo. Worked example: a Resilia song ad, 8:30 long, about 5M views.

## Why song ads work
Long ads (VSLs, natives, advertorials) always sold; the hard part was keeping people there 5-20 minutes, which takes
line-by-line pull most writers can't sustain. A song ad does that for you:
- **The song** carries you with rhythm, humour or emotion. Nobody stops a song halfway.
- **The visuals** change every second, matching today's attention span.
The message underneath is old-school direct response. It's all direct response. Always has been.

**Who wins:** great long-form DR writers (song ads multiply what they do; winning natives turned into songs crushed);
decent writers (the rhythm and visuals carry them through flat lines). **Who loses:** bad DR writers. Great hook
rate, great watch time, no sales: no story, no reason to believe.

## Every ad is one picture
Left: a person stuck with a problem hurting their money, body, mind or relationships. Right: the life they want.
Middle: the product, the bridge. The ad walks them across.
They are scrolling to escape, brain off, next to AI slop and talking dogs. So every ad has two jobs:
1. **Attention** (from avatar research): drama and exact words that stop the scroll (hook rate); a description of
   their life so accurate they think "this is about me" (hold rate).
2. **Belief** (where most ads die): a mechanism (usually the key), a demo (the mechanism shown working), an
   authority figure, social proof, objections answered before they're raised.

## The 10 beats every winning long ad runs on (Resilia, mapped)
| # | Beat | Resilia |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook: the drama, open the loop | "I lost weight before my sister's wedding, and my whole family turned on me." Then stacked conflict: mama called her selfish, sister said she's ruining the day, daddy stopped calling. |
| 2 | Avatar: who she is | "Jasmine is the pretty one... I'm the big one. The one who stands behind her in photos... 'you got such a pretty face.'" Straight from women venting on Reddit about living in someone's shadow. |
| 3 | Problem: living with it | Symptom stacking in her words: "Tired of my face being puffy every morning. Tired of my belly being hard and swollen." |
| 4 | Everything she tried | "You can starve all you want. You can gym all you want." Two lines dismiss every diet. Not lazy, not weak; fighting the wrong enemy. |
| 5 | Messenger | "I called my cousin. She lost about 30 pounds." Someone she trusts who already got the result. |
| 6 | Mechanism | Starts from what she believes (stress: "all those years of being the big sister, carrying everyone"), then the hidden cause and the enemy. "You cannot outrun" it. |
| 7 | Product | One ingredient, one job, a specific dose, a routine ("two softgels a day"). The metaphor (a wall) is shown on screen, then cracked. |
| 8 | Proof | Week 1 the bloat breaks, week 2 sleeps through the night, week 3 belly goes down, then the jeans button. In the order the mechanism promised. |
| 9 | Payoff (longest part) | Mama asks her to "slow down until after the wedding"; the seamstress takes the dress in twice; sister never says congratulations; on the day, daddy looks up and nods. Loop closed. |
| 10 | Close | Her story becomes the viewer's story, then the offer. |

The skeleton is easy to copy; what wins is **hiding it inside a story** (a sister, a wedding, a family turning on
her) so the viewer is watching a drama and never feels sold to, while every beat quietly sells.

**The hook is the theme.** The loop doesn't close in 10 seconds; it runs the whole ad (mama, sister, dress, the
wedding, daddy's nod). 8:30 on one fear: rejection by her own family. The hook works because the result is in the
first second (qualifies the viewer), then a twist (she won and got punished), then a drama word ("turned on me").

**The split:** of 8:30, only 1:51 sells (mechanism, product, proof, offer), about 22 %. 78 % is emotion: shock,
shame, pain, tension, vindication. Story without belief: they cry, comment "beautiful" and scroll. Belief without
story: they never stay to hear it.

## Where strong drama comes from: primal fears
The brain treats anything that could push you out of the tribe, or leave you with nothing, as life or death, and
can't stop watching until it knows how it ends ("could this happen to me?"):
rejection, betrayal, humiliation, losing your place/status, losing your partner, failing to protect your kids,
a health scare (becoming a burden), losing your resources (money, getting scammed).

**Not every fear hits every avatar.** Starting points (research decides):
| Avatar | Fears that land |
|---|---|
| Women 18-29 | Boyfriend cheats or dumps her; friend group leaves her out; she's the "less pretty" one next to a friend or sister |
| Women 30-44 | Husband stops looking at her; mother-in-law judges her; compared to the sister who "has it all together" |
| Women 45-59 | Husband leaves for someone younger; a scary diagnosis; kids leave and she doesn't know who she is |
| Women 60+ | Kids stop letting her see the grandkids; a health scare; scammed out of savings |
| Men 18-29 | A girl leaves him for another guy; friends laugh at him; loses money to a scam |
| Men 30-44 | Wife stops respecting him; laid off; debt piles up and he can't provide |
| Men 45-59 | A heart or weight warning; a younger guy takes his job, place, respect |
| Men 60+ | Falls and can't get up; scammed; kids stop calling |

**The rule that makes drama sell:** the problem (or the result) causes the drama.
- Wrong: a woman in menopause whose husband cheats. Great drama, nothing to do with menopause.
- Right: night sweats so bad she's slept in the guest room for a year so he can rest. One night she goes back to
  apologise; her sister is in her bed. He says: "You moved out of this bed a year ago. What did you expect?" Same
  fear, but the problem emptied her side of the bed. Every scene keeps selling.

## Messenger placement
Common problem (weight, skin, bloating, sleep, joints): the messenger can be close (cousin, sister, coworker,
neighbour). Rare problem: don't force it into the family ("what are the chances?" kills the messenger, then the
mechanism). Put them where she'd naturally meet someone with it: support group, clinic waiting room, Facebook
group, the next salon chair.

## Building it, any product, any format
1. **Research by hand** (AI can pull it; then read it again and again). Six buckets, word for word: their words for
   the problem ("I wake up stiff like I'm 90", not "joint discomfort"); their fears; what they tried and why they
   think it failed; what they believe causes it; the life they want; what stops them buying ("I've wasted so much
   money on this stuff"). That doc is the ad.
2. **Pick the fear, find the scene** where the problem causes it. That scene is the hook and the theme.
3. **Lay out the 10 beats in 10 lines** before writing.
4. **Build the mechanism.** KISS. Believability checklist:
   - Start from what they already believe; build on it, don't fight it.
   - Name the hidden cause underneath, the part nobody told them.
   - Show why everything they tried failed (or they say "I tried that already").
   - Name the enemy, and it's never them.
   - Make it visual: one picture a 10-year-old gets.
   - One ingredient, one job; real doses and routine.
   - Proof in the same order the mechanism predicts.
   - A believable person explains it, not the brand.
   Wrong: "Our formula has glucosamine, turmeric and collagen for joint support." Right: "You've taken glucosamine
   for years. It didn't work because it's rebuilding cartilage while the inflammation keeps eating it. Like patching
   a roof in a storm. Calm the storm first." Test: read it to someone who doesn't know the product; can they explain
   it back? The most complete scientific explanation usually loses.
5. **Outcome and payoff.** Outcome = their life, not "less pain" (on the floor with the grandkid, stairs without the
   rail, yes to the trip). Payoff = the moment the hook's loop closes. Their win first; proving people wrong after
   (revenge is the cherry, not the cake). Flip the hook's hurtful lines at the end; callbacks feel like a reward and
   make people finish. Keep proof honest: "Week one, nothing. I almost quit."
6. **The close.** Call out the viewer with 2-3 research details ("so if you're the one who..."); not your fault;
   the mechanism in one line; the offer. The guarantee answers the biggest objection in the research doc.

**Format doesn't matter.** Same fear, drama and mechanism work as a native ("Why My Own Family Stopped Talking To Me
After I Lost 30 Pounds"), UGC talking head ("Six months ago, my own mama called me selfish..."), or a song.

## The song, last
1. Don't cut the script, adapt it: same story, copy, emotion, details; tweak lines so they can be sung.
2. Verses tell the story; **the chorus carries the mechanism**. By the third chorus they sing it back to themselves.
3. Style for the avatar (age, culture, the emotion each part must hit), not your taste.
4. Clarity beats vibe: every word understood first listen; verses sound like a real person talking. Pretty but
   unclear loses.
5. Visuals show what the words say, every line its own visual; the mechanism gets the most memorable one.

## Don't let AI grade its own ad
Asked "is this good?", it says yes. It won't catch the boring mechanism, the line that doesn't follow, the
messenger nobody believes. Judgment comes from hand research, breaking down winners, and watching a lot of drama.
So we review against the checklists above, line by line, and the user decides.

## Do this today (the article's checklist)
- Map one winning long ad in your niche (30+ days running) to the 10 beats.
- Read 50 avatar venting posts; copy 30 lines word for word.
- Pick the most common fear; write 10 hooks where the problem causes the drama.
- Run the mechanism through the checklist; read it to an outsider.
- Only then, make it a song.
