I almost lost my leg to a swelling everyone kept calling "managed" on a Caribbean cruise. 

The ship's doctor was from Switzerland. She asked me ONE question that made my American doctor look DECADES behind.

I'm 64 years old.

My husband and I had been saving for this trip for almost three years. Caribbean cruise. Ten days. Puerto Rico, St. Thomas, St. Lucia.

We'd talked about this trip since the kids were in college. The kind of trip you plan in your forties for your sixties. The reason you keep showing up to a job you stopped loving years ago.

Day four, we're walking up the cobblestone streets of Charlotte Amalie toward Blackbeard's Castle for sunset.

You've seen the pictures. White and coral colonial buildings climbing the hillside. Red tin rooftops in the sun. The turquoise Caribbean spread out below. Cobblestones underfoot worn smooth by two hundred years of Danish, then American, then tourist feet.

The kind of place you put on a wall when you finally get home.

My husband was walking ahead of me. He had his phone out taking pictures. Laughing at something a vendor had said in broken English.

I was about thirty feet behind him.

I'd noticed my legs starting to go about halfway up. Not the regular heaviness you'd expect from climbing cobblestones in heat. The deep, tight, "my skin can't hold any more" feeling I'd been feeling at home for the last six months every time I stood more than ten minutes at the stove.

I'd been telling myself it was just being on my feet. Just getting older and holding more fluid after a winter indoors.

I'd been telling my husband the same thing.

I stopped on the cobblestones and looked down at my ankles. Pulled up my pant leg. The sock rings on both calves were deep and red. The skin over my left ankle was shiny and tight, already ballooned past the bone. The dark mottled color on my shin — the one my doctor kept saying we were watching — looked darker in the Caribbean light than it had ever looked at home.

They didn't calm down.

The heaviness got worse. My left ankle started to feel like it was filling from the inside, not painful exactly, but like someone was pumping fluid into tissue that had nowhere left to go. I started to sweat through my blouse. I felt the blood drain out of my face.

I sat down on the wall.

I tried to call out to my husband but my voice wouldn't come.

Tourists kept walking around me. Stepping over my feet. Taking pictures of the view behind me.

I sat there with my hand pressed against the worst of the swelling thinking about my mother.

My mother had chronic venous insufficiency that her doctor called managed for nine years. Water pills. Compression. Elevate. Monitor. By 71 the skin on her shin opened into a wound that never closed. She spent four months in a wound clinic. They took that leg below the knee. She never walked the same. She never looked the same.

I was 38 when that happened.

And here I was, 64 years old, on a Caribbean cobblestone wall two thousand miles from home, staring at the same kind of ankles she'd had the year before they cut.

My husband turned around.

I will never forget his face. He'd been laughing one second. The next second he dropped his phone on the cobblestones and ran back to me.

'Caroline. Caroline what's happening.'

I couldn't speak.

He grabbed my arm. Looked down. Saw the sock rings. Saw the shiny tight skin across both ankles. Saw the dark mottling climbing my shin. He yelled for someone to help. A local man in his sixties from a nearby cafe came running with a chair. They got me into the chair. Somebody brought water. Somebody got ice wrapped in a towel against my ankle.

The cafe owner called for a vehicle. We're in the old town of Charlotte Amalie. The streets are too narrow for ambulances to move fast. They had a small cart that could navigate the cobblestones. They got me onto it and drove us down to the port.

My husband held my hand the whole way down. He didn't say anything. Just held my hand and looked at me with the face I had been seeing in nightmares for twenty-six years since my mother's amputation.

We got onto the tender boat back to the ship. He kept his hand on my calf the whole way across the water.

I told him I was okay.

I wasn't.
